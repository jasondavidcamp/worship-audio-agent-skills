# Arrangement And Dynamics Diagnosis

Use this when the mix feels dense, flat, tiring, lyric-masked, or emotionally static even though individual channel tones seem acceptable.

## Source Material Mined

- Church Production, "Mixing Techniques": worship mix approaches differ by style, but process, vocal priority, foundation, density, and space matter.
- Sweetwater, "How to Play Compatibly in a Worship Band": players must leave musical room and choose parts that work together.
- Worship Online, "The Complete Guide to Worship Drumming": worship drumming depends on serving the song, dynamic contrast, click/groove reliability, and section-aware restraint.

## Core Principle

Not every mix problem is a mix move. A crowded arrangement can hide the lyric, flatten the service arc, and make every section feel the same before the engineer touches a fader.

## Triage Questions

- Who owns the lyric, rhythm, harmonic bed, hook, and low-end foundation in this section?
- Are piano left hand, acoustic guitar, electric guitars, pads, tracks, and BGVs occupying the same midrange?
- Does the verse leave space for the lyric, or is it already arranged like a chorus?
- Does the chorus lift through performance, arrangement, and dynamics, or only through fader level?
- Are cymbals, fills, strums, and pads filling every gap?
- Are tracks competing with live players instead of supporting them?
- Does the band have planned dynamic levels for intro, verse, pre-chorus, chorus, bridge, and response?

## Diagnostic Patterns

- Vocal is loud but still hard to follow: lyric lane is masked by midrange arrangement density, not only low vocal level.
- Big chorus does not feel big: verse/pre-chorus may already be too full, leaving no contrast.
- Mix feels tiring: constant cymbals, full strumming, dense pads, and BGVs may create continuous energy with no rest.
- Bridge loses emotional focus: too many melodic/harmonic parts may compete with the lead vocal or congregational line.
- Drums feel too loud but disappear when lowered: drummer part may need dynamic shaping, not only fader reduction.
- Acoustic/piano sounds good solo but muddies the band: useful solo tone may not be useful arrangement tone.

## Lowest-Risk Moves

- Identify the section's main job before changing tone: lyric, build, release, response, or congregational support.
- Make fader moves by role: lead vocal and lyric support first, foundation second, texture/hook third.
- Use section automation or snapshots to create contrast rather than one static balance for the whole song.
- Carve arrangement lanes with small EQ/fader choices: less piano low-mid, less acoustic body, narrower pads, fewer BGVs in verses.
- If you can influence the band, ask for part changes in musical language: "leave the verse thinner," "save full strum for chorus," "let piano and acoustic alternate motion."
- Treat drum dynamics as arrangement data: rim/cross-stick, closed hats, ghost notes, full snare, ride/crash, and fills should mark section size.
- Distinguish "console move" from "band-director note" in the recommendation.

## Output Shape

When this playbook applies, produce:

- Arrangement diagnosis: what is overcrowding or under-supporting the section.
- Console move: what the engineer can safely do now.
- Team note: what the worship leader, musician, or arranger could change later.
- Section goal: how the next verse/chorus/bridge should feel different.
