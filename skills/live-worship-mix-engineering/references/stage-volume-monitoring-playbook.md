# Stage Volume And Monitoring Playbook

Use this when FOH clarity, livestream balance, vocal intelligibility, drum control, feedback margin, or musician confidence is being shaped by what is happening on stage before the mix reaches the PA.

## Source Material Mined

- Yamaha, "Optimizing Your Monitor Mix and Avoiding Feedback": lower onstage sound improves FOH, IEMs reduce mic pickup and feedback, and FOH should be built before monitor mixes.
- QSC, "House of Worship Audio Training: Monitor Mixing 101": monitoring affects timing, pitch, confidence, stage volume, and soundcheck workflow.
- Aviom, "Lowering Stage Volume & Improving Worship": personal monitoring can reduce sanctuary spill and give the engineer more control over the congregation mix.
- Shure feedback guidance: open mics, mic/loudspeaker distance, directional patterns, and monitor placement affect gain before feedback.

## Core Principle

Stage volume is part of the source. If the room is hearing cymbals, guitar amps, wedges, and acoustic drums directly, the console may only be adding clarity or reinforcement, not controlling the whole sound.

## Triage Questions

- Are musicians on wedges, IEMs, amps, acoustic instruments, or a hybrid?
- Is the audience hearing stage sound louder than the PA in the first rows or smaller rooms?
- Are vocal mics aimed away from wedge energy and PA spill?
- Are singers asking for "more me" because the mix is missing useful reference, or because the stage is too loud?
- Does the stream sound thin because FOH is not adding much of the sources that are already loud in the room?
- Are drum/cymbal choices, stick choice, shield use, or drummer dynamics controlling room loudness?
- Are IEM mixes helping performance, or isolating players enough that they overplay or lose connection?

## Diagnostic Patterns

- Vocal cannot get loud enough before feedback: monitor level/placement, mic technique, open mics, or PA geometry may be the real limit.
- FOH vocal feels present in headphones but buried in room: stage wash, cymbal spill, guitar amps, or wedges may be masking the PA.
- Stream sounds vocal-heavy but room sounds band-heavy: acoustic stage volume is replacing PA band level in the room.
- Drums are pokey but not powerful: cymbals/snare may dominate acoustically while kick/toms need controlled PA support.
- Musicians keep requesting more level: they may need a clearer individual monitor mix, not simply a louder shared wedge.
- Singers have pitch issues: monitor/IEM content may be missing their own voice, the key instrument, or reliable pitch reference.

## Lowest-Risk Moves

- Build input gain and FOH tone first, then monitors, so monitor requests are based on usable source sounds.
- Reduce stage level at the source: quieter amp placement, less wedge level, IEM discipline, controlled cymbals, lighter sticks, or better drum dynamics.
- Prefer individual monitor mixes over one shared loud wedge when possible.
- Keep wedge mixes simple and feedback-safe; avoid musician-controlled wedge sends unless the system is protected.
- For IEMs, give players useful timing and pitch anchors: own source, lead vocal, click/cue if used, bass/kick, and one harmonic instrument.
- Use mute groups/DCAs to reduce open mics and unused stage noise between songs and speech segments.
- When FOH and stream disagree, explicitly account for acoustic stage sound in the room but not in the stream.

## Volunteer-Safe Coaching Language

- "The mix is not only a console problem; the stage is currently part of the PA."
- "Before adding more vocal level, let us reduce what is fighting it."
- "A clearer monitor mix can often be quieter than a louder unclear one."
- "For the stream, we need to replace the acoustic energy the room gets for free."

## Skill Behavior

When this playbook applies, distinguish:

- Monitor fix: improves musician confidence and performance.
- Stage-volume fix: lowers spill and masking in the room.
- FOH fix: balances what the PA is responsible for.
- Stream fix: adds what the stream is missing because it cannot hear the room.
