# Target Mix Hierarchy

Use this when turning worship values into practical mix priorities, fader decisions, and critique language.

Mined from public source:

- Cross Point Church, Audio Engineer Guide: https://crosspoint.tv/wp-content/uploads/2022/07/AudioEngineerGuide_2022.pdf

This is a public church target-mix document. Treat it as a useful example of worship mix hierarchy, not as a universal standard.

## First Principle

The mix should guide attention. In worship, the words and congregational participation matter more than impressive processing.

## Default Hierarchy

1. Message and lyrics.
2. Lead vocal.
3. Musical foundation that supports singing.
4. BGVs/choir/congregational cues.
5. Energy, width, and polish.

If a mix move improves polish but weakens the message or lyric clarity, reject or reduce it.

## Practical Fader Picture

Lead vocal:

- Clearly out front.
- Emotionally connected, not pasted on.
- Warm enough to feel human, clear enough to understand.
- Controlled sibilance and harshness.

BGVs and choir:

- Audible enough to encourage singing.
- Tucked behind lead unless they carry a call-and-response or congregational cue.
- Reverb can help place them behind the lead, but too much creates blur.

Snare and drums:

- Snare can sit close behind the lead vocal for energy.
- Drums should create motion without turning the service into a drum feature.
- Cymbal energy must not fight vocal intelligibility.

Bass and kick:

- Decide the relationship before boosting low end.
- Kick can provide attack and pulse.
- Bass can provide sustained weight and warmth.
- The blend should feel supportive, not sub-heavy by default.

Band:

- Full enough to carry worship energy.
- Not so loud or dense that it becomes performance-forward instead of congregation-forward.
- Use keys/piano/guitars to support warmth and harmonic body, but leave a lane for vocals.

Effects:

- Lead vocal reverb/delay should support emotion and space.
- Mute or reduce music effects during speech.
- Embellish after the natural source is clear.

## Diagnosis Language

Use target language like:

- "Vocals are leading the room."
- "The band supports the congregation without crowding the lyric."
- "The low end feels warm and anchored, not oversized."
- "The chorus lifts without becoming abrasive."
- "BGVs make the room feel invited to sing."

Avoid vague praise like "sounds professional" unless the specific hierarchy is working.

## Conflict Rules

If vocal clarity conflicts with band size, vocal clarity wins.

If warmth conflicts with lyric intelligibility, reduce masking before adding top.

If low-end impact conflicts with small-speaker or livestream translation, reduce low end and preserve the message.

If BGVs feel beautiful but confuse the lead lyric, tuck or carve them.

If reverb creates emotion but pushes the singer away, reduce wet level, shorten decay, or increase pre-delay.

## Skill Behavior

When critiquing a mix, name the attention hierarchy explicitly:

```text
The mix is energetic, but the hierarchy is inverted: drums and keys pull more attention than the lead vocal. First move is not master loudness; it is restoring the vocal lane and tucking the cymbal/keys energy so the lyric leads again.
```
